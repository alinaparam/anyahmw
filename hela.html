The woman whose cells never died

The story of Henrietta Lacks and the first immortal human cell line — and the questions it still asks us.

In 1951, a young woman called Henrietta Lacks came to Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, USA. She was 30 years old and had five children. She grew up on a tobacco farm in Virginia, and she loved cooking, dancing and painting her nails red. For some weeks she felt a strange pain inside her body. The doctors found a tumour: it was cervical cancer.

During her treatment, a doctor took a small piece of the tumour. Nobody asked Henrietta, and nobody told her. At that time, this was normal: doctors did not need a patient’s consent to use tissue for research. The sample went to the lab of Dr George Gey. For years, Gey and his team tried to grow human cells outside the body, but the cells always died after a few days.

Henrietta’s cells were different. They did not die. They divided again and again, and the number of cells doubled about every 24 hours. Gey called them HeLa, from the first letters of her first name and her surname. It was the first immortal human cell line. Sadly, Henrietta did not live to see it. The cancer spread very fast, and she died in October 1951. She was only 31.

Gey shared HeLa cells with other scientists for free, and soon labs all over the world were growing them. HeLa cells helped scientists to test the polio vaccine in the 1950s. They helped to prove that a virus, HPV, can cause cervical cancer, and that discovery later won a Nobel Prize. Scientists used HeLa to study cancer, genes and radiation; they even sent HeLa cells into space. Later, companies started to sell the cells and made a lot of money.

But there was a problem nobody expected. HeLa cells grow so fast and so easily that they got into other cultures. In the 1960s and 70s, scientists discovered that many “different” cell lines in many labs were really HeLa. This contamination ruined years of research, and it is one reason why labs today check the identity of their cell lines.

And Henrietta’s family? For more than twenty years they knew nothing. In 1973, scientists called them and asked for blood samples, because they wanted to study HeLa genes. The family was shocked: part of their mother was alive in labs everywhere, and companies were selling it, but they were poor and often could not pay for their own doctors.

The story became famous in 2010, when the journalist Rebecca Skloot wrote the book “The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks”. In 2013, scientists published the HeLa genome online — again without asking the family. After that, the National Institutes of Health made an agreement: now members of the Lacks family help to decide who can use the genome data. In 2023, the family reached an agreement with a big biotech company that sold HeLa cells.

Today, HeLa cells are still in thousands of labs. Henrietta Lacks died more than seventy years ago, but in a way she is still with us — in every flask of HeLa cells, and in every question about science, consent and fairness.
