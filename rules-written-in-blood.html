Lab safety · a short history

Rules written in blood

Why do labs have so many rules? Because behind many of them there is a real person and a real story.

Every lab has rules. You must wear gloves. You mustn’t eat or drink. You have to write a label on every tube. Sometimes these rules look boring, and new students ask: “Why do I have to do this?” Scientists have a dark answer: “Lab rules are written in blood.” It means that many rules appeared after somebody got sick or died. Here are some of their stories.

In 1898, Marie Curie and her husband Pierre discovered two new elements: polonium and radium. Radium was beautiful: it glowed in the dark. Marie often carried small tubes of radium in her pocket and kept them in her desk. Nobody knew how dangerous radiation was. She died in 1934, and doctors think that radiation made her ill. Her notebooks are still radioactive today, and they will be radioactive for about 1,500 more years. They are in a library in Paris, in special boxes. If you want to read them, you have to sign a document and wear protective clothes.

Radioactive materials
You must keep them in a special box, not in your pocket.

At that time, radium was very popular. In the 1920s, young women in American factories painted numbers on watches with radium paint, so people could see the time at night. Their bosses told them to make the brush sharp with their lips. For fun, some women even painted their nails and teeth with the paint. Many of them got very sick, and some died. They went to court, and their story changed the safety rules for workers in the USA.

No food, no drinks, no make-up
You mustn’t put anything in your mouth at work.

For many years, scientists pipetted by mouth: they used a glass tube like a drinking straw. It was fast and easy, but sometimes the liquid went into their mouths: bacteria, viruses, chemicals. Many lab workers got infections this way. Today the rule is simple: you mustn’t pipette by mouth. You have to use a pipette with a button. The same is true for food: you mustn’t eat, drink or put on make-up in the lab, because your hands can carry chemicals to your mouth.

Mouth pipetting
You mustn’t do it. Never!

In 1996, Karen Wetterhahn, a chemistry professor in the USA, was working with a very toxic chemical, dimethylmercury. She wore gloves, of course. But one or two drops fell on her glove. She didn’t feel anything. Five months later she started to feel strange: she couldn’t walk well, and she couldn’t speak clearly. Scientists found that the chemical went through her glove in a few seconds. She died in 1997. After her death, the rules changed: now people have to use special gloves for this kind of work, and many labs don’t use dimethylmercury at all.

The right gloves
You have to check which gloves you need.

Of course, not every rule has a sad story. You don’t have to wear a lab coat in the kitchen, and you don’t have to learn all the rules by heart: there is a poster on the wall. But next time a rule looks boring or silly, take into account that maybe somebody paid a very high price for it.
